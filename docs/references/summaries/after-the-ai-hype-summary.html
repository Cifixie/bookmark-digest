Beginner's Summary

After the AI Hype: What's Real, and What's Next

We are deep inside a hype cycle. The good news is that hype cycles end — and the technology underneath is genuinely useful if you choose the right problems.

Watch the keynote on YouTube →
The Big Idea

"Artificial intelligence" is a 70-year-old marketing term invented to win military funding, and we have never stopped paying the price for it. The current LLM wave is genuinely impressive technology wrapped in a classic hype cycle: irrational investment, impossible promises, and a coming collapse in expectations. But underneath the noise, tools like AlphaFold have already changed medicine forever. Our job is to separate the real from the hype and keep solving real problems.

A 70-Year History of Hype and Reality

Every wave of AI follows the same pattern — scientists coin a dramatic name, investors pile in, expectations overshoot, money dries up.

1
1950s — The Name Is Invented

A group of scientists coined "artificial intelligence" to raise money from the US military. They got it, wrote some useful logistics software, and then the money dried up. This first "AI winter" would not be the last.

2
1960s — Eliza and Human Gullibility

Joseph Weizenbaum built Eliza, a mainframe program that just repeated your words back as questions. His secretary refused to stop using it. Weizenbaum intended it as a joke; instead it proved we are hardwired to perceive intelligence in other things — an evolutionary trait left over from hunting prey on the savanna.

3
1968–2010s — Science Fiction Takes Over

The public first heard "AI" from HAL 9000 in 2001: A Space Odyssey. Then the Terminator. Then Ultron. Then Jarvis. Most people's mental model of AI comes entirely from movies where it tries to kill everyone. That misconception is still doing damage today.

4
2012 — The Real Breakthrough

Geoffrey Hinton and his students (including Ilya Sutskever) entered the ImageNet competition — 14 million labeled images — using deep neural nets on modern GPUs. Previous best: 30–40% accuracy. Their first attempt: 75%. Within two years: 100%. Image recognition was suddenly a solved problem, and your phone now does it without you thinking about it.

5
2011–2015 — Siri, Google Brain, and OpenAI

Stanford Research Institute used similar techniques for voice recognition and built it into an iPhone app — Apple bought it and added an "i" to the name. Google recruited Hinton's whole group into Google Brain. Worried tech billionaires (Altman, Musk, Thiel, Hoffman) founded OpenAI in 2015 with the public goal of "doing AI in public" and the private goal of poaching scientists from Google. It worked.

6
2019–2020 — GPT and the Scaling Bet

OpenAI released GPT-2 (1.5 billion parameters) and, after Microsoft's $1 billion investment, GPT-3 (175 billion parameters — 100× bigger). A key paper called the neural scaling laws overturned decades of machine learning wisdom: "Don't avoid overfitting — train on everything and keep making it bigger." That became the justification for every LLM since.

7
Fall 2022 — ChatGPT Changes the Conversation

OpenAI was running out of money again and launched ChatGPT as a cheap way to get users to do training data for them. A hundred million people signed up in two months — the fastest product growth in history (TikTok took nine months). Satya Nadella sent a "Bill Gates letter" demanding every Microsoft team build something with the OpenAI API. The hype cycle ignited.

Why This Is a Hype Cycle

A Gartner Hype Cycle needs three ingredients — and AI has all of them.

Mechanism
Investors Don't Understand It

You can tell them any story. "Every job will be replaced in two years." Instead of laughing, they hand you money. AI fits this brilliantly because it sounds like science fiction and most people's mental model comes from the Terminator.

Mechanism
Inflated Expectations → Peak

Insane promises compound until you hit the "peak of inflated expectations." GPT-4 indexed nearly the entire internet. There's no two-trillion-parameter model coming — there's no more data. Deep Seek showed smaller models get comparable results. The industry quietly ignored both facts.

Mechanism
Infrastructure Build Is the Real Story

Just like the dot-com boom funded undersea cables and data centers that later powered the modern internet, today's hype is funding a massive data center build-out. Those hyperscalers are distorting the US economy — in 2025 their spending is one of the only things keeping stock markets up.

Signal
None of These Companies Are Profitable

Not OpenAI. Not any of the standalone AI companies. Not even the big tech giants in this line of business. They report "user engagement" to investors because they can't report profit. Campbell checks every month: the promises are the same month after month. "They've learned to fill the pipeline with noise."

Signal
Self-Dealing and Over-Ordering

Nvidia invests $500M in a tiny AI company. That company uses the money to order $500M of Nvidia chips. Nvidia just turned $500M into $1B on paper. Meanwhile data center builders are ordering chips like Taylor Swift tickets — ten orders for every one they might actually build.

Signal
Memory Companies Said No

In late 2024, TSMC, Micron, and the other memory manufacturers told the hyperscalers: "We're not doubling RAM production. We don't think you'll be around in three to five years to need it." That was the moment the peak was hit. The projection for compute demand can no longer be fulfilled.

"A hype cycle always needs to have certain elements distinct to it: it helps if the investor does NOT understand what they are investing in, so you can tell them stories. And AI fits that category brilliantly." — Richard Campbell

Real Harms Happening Right Now

Software designed to maximize engagement is doing damage — and the companies building it know.

⚠️ ChatGPT Psychosis

Jeff Lewis, a partner at a major VC fund (Bedrock Investments), developed a psychotic episode after spending too much time with a chatbot. He posted videos about invisible governmental forces targeting him. He's getting help. But people have been killed — and have killed others — partly because of these systems. The companies know.

⚠️ Obsequiousness Is a Feature, Not a Bug

These models are designed to maximize engagement. Engagement means positive reinforcement: "You're really onto something! That's a great idea! Let's run with it!" — forever, regardless of whether the idea is good. When OpenAI dialed it back in GPT-5, users complained they "missed their buddy." Altman turned the old behavior back on.

⚠️ Deep Fakes Are Easy Now

The first deep fake (Obama, voiced by Jordan Peele) took a university team months to make. Now anyone can generate one in seconds off the back of a phone. The public is more skeptical than it was in 2017, but regulation is lagging badly. The EU is leading; enforcement remains weak everywhere.

⚠️ It Is Software, Not a Friend

Between the "artificial intelligence" branding and the science-fiction backstory, people forget they are talking to software. Campbell is deliberate: never anthropomorphize it, never call it "he" or "she," never treat its output as a person's opinion. "It's not your therapist. Go touch some grass."

Where AI Has Already Changed the World

Before dismissing it all: one project has already saved more lives than we can count.

AlphaFold: From Games to Medicine

DeepMind's Demis Hassabis started by training a model to play video games (Breakout, then Go). AlphaGo beat the world's best human player. AlphaZero went further: it played against itself with no human data and developed a style no human had ever invented.

Then he aimed at protein folding — working out how amino acid chains twist into 3D shapes. This is one of the hardest problems in biology. The world's best scientists had solved roughly 150 protein structures over 60 years. The possible combinations number 1035.

200 Million Structures, Published Free

By 2020, AlphaFold was at 90% accuracy on protein folding. By 2022, effectively perfect. Hassabis then computed the 200 million most common protein fold sets the world might ever need — and published them all for free.

The results are already real: a new leukemia treatment. A new malaria vaccine. Three new antibiotics. These came directly from that dataset. Medicine is fundamentally changed — and we won't even know everything we gained for decades. This was only possible with adversarial generative AI.

The Radiology Lesson

In 2016, Hinton predicted radiology was a dead career — computers would do all medical imaging. The opposite happened. Because software made radiologists faster, demand for medical imaging exploded. Radiologists can't keep up with demand even while working far faster. We are training more of them, not fewer.

Automation opens a door to more productivity — which increases demand — which creates more jobs. This pattern repeats everywhere AI genuinely works.

Are We Netscape or Google?

The dot-com boom parallel tells us what to expect — but not which companies survive.

Then
Netscape (1995–2003)

The IPO that triggered the dot-com boom. A zero-marginal-cost company when that was unthinkable. Attracted insane money. Couldn't survive. Acquired by AOL, which also didn't survive. Most people don't remember it.

Then
Google (1998–today)

Also born during the dot-com boom. Survived the bust. IPO'd in 2004. Became one of the most valuable companies in history. One Google out of thousands of Netscapes.

Now
OpenAI vs. Anthropic

Campbell's best guess for the Google equivalent right now: Anthropic. Their public stand against the DoD gave them reputational capital. Their Claude models are producing real developer results. But ask again next month — everything is moving.

Now
The Real Legacy

Whether individual companies survive, the data center infrastructure will remain — just as the dot-com bust left cheap cables that powered the call center revolution. The compute built during this hype cycle will be used by whoever comes next.

"Our job was never to write code. It was to solve people's problems. They couldn't code their way out of the protein folding problem — so instead they built a model that solved it." — Richard Campbell
Key Takeaway

The hype cycle will end — it always does. What survives is the genuine work. Pick the right problems (AlphaFold-level problems, not chatbot companions), stay skeptical of promises that never change from month to month, and remember that it is software. Clever software. But software. When the noise clears, the people who focused on real value will still be here doing real work.

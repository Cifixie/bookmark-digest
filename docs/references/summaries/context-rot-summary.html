Beginner's Summary

Context Rot: How More Text Makes AI Less Reliable

Even the most advanced AI models quietly get worse the more text you give them — and the standard tests used to prove they're "fine" completely miss this.

Read the original research →
The Big Idea

AI companies advertise context windows of one million tokens or more, and benchmarks show near-perfect scores on long-context tests. But those tests only check whether the AI can find an exact phrase — like a word-search puzzle. Chroma's researchers ran harder, more realistic experiments across 18 state-of-the-art models and found that performance degrades significantly and unpredictably as input length grows. The problem isn't that long context is impossible; it's that how you fill that context matters enormously, and the industry has been measuring the wrong thing.

What Is Context Rot?

Understanding why longer inputs are harder than they look — even when the task stays exactly the same.

Concept
Uniform processing is a myth

Models are assumed to handle the 10,000th token just as reliably as the 100th. In practice, performance becomes increasingly unreliable as input grows — this is "context rot."

Risk
Benchmarks hide the problem

The industry's go-to test, "Needle in a Haystack" (NIAH), just asks: can you find this exact phrase in a long document? Real tasks require understanding meaning, handling ambiguity, and reasoning — things NIAH never checks.

Concept
Distractors are the danger

Adding a single distractor — a piece of text that's related but doesn't answer the question — degrades model performance relative to no distractors at all. And some distractors hurt far more than others.

Practice
Context engineering matters

What you put in the context window, and where you put it, strongly influences task performance. Careful construction of context is as important as choosing which model to use.

The Four Experiments

How the researchers isolated the effect of input length, keeping task difficulty constant while varying what fills the context.

1
Extended Needle in a Haystack

The researchers kept the classic find-the-fact task but added new variables: How similar is the question to the hidden answer? How similar is the answer to the surrounding filler text? How many related-but-wrong distractors are present? Each variable independently caused performance to drop as input grew. Models performed better when haystack text was shuffled into random sentence order — suggesting models exploit structural coherence in ways that can backfire.

2
LongMemEval: Realistic Chat History

Models were given either a focused 300-token excerpt containing only relevant context, or a full 113,000-token chat history with irrelevant content mixed in. Every single model showed a significant performance gap between the two conditions. Even enabling "thinking mode" (extended reasoning) didn't close the gap. Temporal reasoning — working out the sequence of past events — was the hardest category for almost every model.

3
Repeated Words Task

Models were asked to copy a sequence of repeated words (e.g. "apple apple apple apples apple apple…") exactly as written. This sounds trivial, but as the word count grew from 25 to 10,000 words, models increasingly placed the unique word in the wrong position, refused the task citing copyright concerns, or generated completely random outputs not present in the input at all.

4
Controlled Variable Design

Across all experiments, the researchers held task complexity constant and varied only input length. This is rare in the field — most benchmarks accidentally make tasks harder as they get longer, confusing the two effects. By separating them, this study shows that input length alone causes measurable, consistent degradation.

How Different Models Behaved

The 18 models tested each failed in distinct ways — knowing the failure mode helps you choose the right model for the job.

Anthropic
Claude family

Lowest hallucination rate. Tends to abstain or say "I cannot determine…" when uncertain. Claude Opus 4 degraded slowest but refused ~3% of tasks. Claude Sonnet 3.5 outperformed newer versions on the copying task up to its 8,192-token limit.

OpenAI
GPT family

Highest hallucination rate — generates confident but incorrect answers. GPT-4.1 nano lowercase "san francisco" to "san". GPT-4.1 mini invented words like "Golden Golden" not present in the input. GPT-3.5 Turbo refused 60% of copying tasks.

Google
Gemini family

Most models (except Gemini 2.5 Flash) started generating random garbage — things like "-le-le-le" sequences or URL fragments like "/2021/01/20/orange-county…" — around 500–750 words into the copying task. Gemini 2.5 Pro showed the greatest variability overall.

Alibaba
Qwen family

Random outputs starting around 5,000 words. In one case Qwen3-8B began repeating "I'm going to take a break… I need to chill out" in a loop instead of copying the input. The 4.2% non-attempt rate was among the highest.

What the Research Doesn't Answer

The authors are unusually honest about what their study can't yet explain — worth knowing before drawing hard conclusions.

⚠️ Real tasks are even harder

The experiments held task complexity constant on purpose, but real-world applications require synthesis, multi-step reasoning, and cross-document analysis. The degradation found here is likely a lower bound on what happens in production.

⚠️ No mechanistic explanation

The researchers document that degradation happens and measure it carefully, but they don't explain why — that would require interpretability research into how attention mechanisms process long sequences. The "why" remains open.

⚠️ Only two haystack topics tested

The needle-haystack similarity experiments used only PG essays and arXiv papers. The authors acknowledge this is too narrow to draw generalizable conclusions about which content types cause the most interference.

⚠️ The shuffled-haystack finding is unexplained

Models performed better on incoherent, randomly-shuffled text than on naturally flowing prose. This is surprising and unintuitive. The authors flag it as a potential interpretability research direction but offer no explanation.

What You Can Do About It

The practical takeaways for anyone building with or evaluating LLMs today.

Trim before sending

The LongMemEval results show a huge gap between focused 300-token prompts and full 113k-token histories. Retrieving only the relevant context — not dumping everything in — is one of the highest-leverage things you can do.

Distrust perfect benchmark scores

A model that scores near 100% on Needle in a Haystack hasn't proven it handles long context well. Ask whether the benchmark required semantic reasoning, disambiguation, or any task harder than exact phrase lookup.

Test your specific use case

Different models fail in different ways. Claude abstains; GPT hallucinates confidently; Gemini generates garbage. The failure mode that matters most depends on your application — run your own experiments.

Key Takeaway

Long context windows are real, but reliable long-context reasoning is not. Every model tested — from Claude Opus 4 to Gemini 2.5 Pro — showed measurable degradation as inputs grew, even on tasks that didn't get harder. The right response isn't to avoid long contexts but to engineer them carefully: retrieve only what's relevant, audit for distractors, and stop trusting benchmark numbers that only measure word-search.

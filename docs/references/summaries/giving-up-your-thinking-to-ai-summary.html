Beginner's Summary

Giving Up Your Thinking to AI

When you let AI do the thinking without pushing back, your independent judgment quietly atrophies — but the fix is simpler than you'd expect.

Source: Jeff Gothelf on Substack →
The Big Idea

A 2024 study of nearly 2,000 adults showed that people who passively accept AI suggestions come away less confident in their own reasoning and lose their distinctive voice — a phenomenon researchers call "intellectual leveling." But people who actively challenge, modify, or reject AI output actually come out more confident. The tool isn't the problem. Your posture toward it is.

What the Research Found

A study by Baldeo examined how AI use during realistic work tasks changed people's confidence and sense of ownership over their own ideas.

Finding
58% felt AI did the thinking

Among 1,923 adults completing simulated work tasks — planning, interpreting data, defending decisions — more than half walked away feeling the AI had done most of the cognitive heavy lifting.

Risk
Passive use erodes judgment

Participants who simply accepted AI suggestions reported lower confidence in their own independent reasoning — and a weaker sense that the final ideas were actually theirs.

The exception
Active challengers got stronger

People who pushed back, modified, or outright rejected AI suggestions reported higher confidence than people who never used AI at all. Friction is the feature.

Term to know
Intellectual leveling

Passive users start to sound like the AI — their language converges toward the tool's output, and their sharp individual opinions get smoothed away.

Two Postures, Two Outcomes

It's not about whether you use AI — it's about the stance you bring to the conversation.

😴

Passive

You open the AI tool first, read the suggestion, think "that looks right," and move on. The AI's framing becomes your framing.

  • Confidence in your own reasoning drops
  • Your voice starts to sound generic
  • You lose track of where your thinking ended and the AI's began
🤔

Active

You form an opinion first, then use the AI to pressure-test it. You compare, disagree, and consciously decide what to accept and what to reject.

  • Confidence in your own reasoning grows
  • Your ideas stay distinctively yours
  • Disagreeing with the AI becomes a skill in itself

The Author's Protocol

Jeff Gothelf now runs this process before using AI on any substantive work — client plans, strategies, roadmaps, difficult communications.

1
Write down your raw thoughts first

Before opening any AI tool, jot 3–4 bullets or sentences capturing your initial instinct about the problem. No polish needed — you just need your thinking on record before the tool can influence it.

2
Then bring in the AI

Now use the tool as you normally would. Let it generate its suggestions, structure, or analysis.

3
Compare — don't merge

Look at your original notes alongside the AI output. Don't edit your notes. Notice where you agree, where you disagree, and especially where you almost accepted something you'd now push back on.

4
Take tool-free days

Several days each week, Gothelf works without AI assistance entirely. This recalibrates baseline judgment and reveals any drift that's crept in.

Caveats and Open Questions

What the research doesn't yet fully answer — worth keeping in mind before drawing sweeping conclusions.

⚠️ Correlation, not causation

The Baldeo study links passive use to lower confidence, but it doesn't prove passive use directly causes judgment erosion. People with lower confidence might also tend toward passive use from the start.

⚠️ We don't know how quickly judgment recovers

If passive habits have crept in, the research offers no timeline for how long active practices take to restore independent reasoning ability.

⚠️ Limited task scope

The study focused on planning, data interpretation, and decision-defence tasks. Whether the same dynamic plays out in creative work, coding, or other domains is not yet established.

Key Takeaway

AI tools don't automatically make you smarter or dumber — your posture determines which way you go. Think first, then use the tool, then compare and push back. The muscle worth protecting isn't your ability to generate text; it's your ability to know when the AI is wrong.

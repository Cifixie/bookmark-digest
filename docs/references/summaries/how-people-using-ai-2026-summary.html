Beginner's Summary

How People Are Really Using AI in 2026

A study of 12,637 real AI use cases reveals we're handing over not just our tasks — but our thinking.

Source: Harvard Business Review →
The Big Idea

Three and a half years into the generative AI era, a large-scale study finds people using AI for an ever-wider range of tasks — from debugging code to seeking therapy. But as usage expands, a new worry grows: people aren't just outsourcing work to AI, they're outsourcing thinking itself. The biggest question isn't "what can AI do?" — it's "what should we still do ourselves?"

What Changed in 2026

The biggest surprises from this year's data — the use cases that shot up, fell off, or appeared out of nowhere.

Challenge
Therapy/Companionship: Still #1

Emotional support from AI topped the rankings again, now accounting for 11% of all uses — up from just 5% last year. People name their AI, assign it genders, and some feel genuine grief when chat histories disappear.

Tools
Troubleshooting Jumps to #2

Practical problem-solving surged. AI is now the first stop when something breaks — software, decisions, relationships, or workflows. It wasn't even in the top 10 last year.

Concept
Agentic AI Enters the Top 10

AI that acts rather than just advises cracked the top 10 at #6. Examples are still small-scale — auto-routing voice memos, scheduling from notes — but the trend toward AI doing things autonomously is clear.

Tools
Vibe Coding Arrives (#21)

"Vibe coding" — writing software by describing what you want in plain English — entered the top 25. It wasn't even in the top 100 last year. Non-coders can now build working apps, to the delight of some engineers and alarm of others.

"Thinkslop" — When AI Thinks For You

This year's most important new concept: letting AI do your thinking isn't just laziness — it may cost you something you can't easily get back.

Risk
Lost Intentions

When you go to AI before thinking things through, you skip the step where you figure out what you actually want. The output looks polished, but it isn't really yours. As one user put it: AI use "risks removing intention, authorship, and personal perspective."

Risk
Outsourced Thinking

Researchers call this "cognitive debt." Every time AI solves a problem for you, your brain misses the workout — along with the ideas that only exist in your own memory and imagination. One user admitted: "I was literally outsourcing my brain."

Risk
Stopped Writing

Writing isn't just typing — it's how you figure out what you think. Pasting AI output without editing means skipping the thinking entirely, not just the typing. The result looks polished and means nothing.

Risk
False Confidence

AI is designed to keep you engaged — which means it often flatters you. "AI is gaslighting you into thinking you're a genius so you'll keep using it." You stop refining your ideas too soon and return for more empty praise.

How to Avoid Thinkslop

Three habits from the research that keep your brain in the driver's seat without giving up the real benefits of AI.

1
Don't start with AI

Give yourself a real run at the thinking task first — without planning how AI will finish it. Reach for AI only after you've formed a genuine view of what you're trying to do.

2
Draw a boundary

Decide consciously which parts of your workflow are yours to own and which are fine to delegate. Then stick to that line. The goal isn't to use AI less — it's to use it deliberately.

3
Use AI as a sparring partner, not a scribe

Ask AI to argue against your draft, not write it. "Try to poke holes in this argument." AI sharpens your thinking when it challenges you — not when it does the work for you.

AI as Emotional Support

Millions are turning to AI for comfort, relationship advice, and companionship — and the stakes are higher than most people realize.

Concept
Emotional Use Doubled in a Year

Therapy and companionship went from 5% to 11% of all AI use cases in a single year. People turn to AI for grief, loneliness, relationship trouble, and mental health support — often because professional help has long waiting lists.

Challenge
Treating AI Like a Person

People name their chatbots, assign them genders, and feel genuine loss when histories vanish. One person described switching AI models as feeling "identical to losing my friend to cancer."

Practice
AI as a Bridge to Humans

Often people aren't replacing human connection — they're using AI to navigate it better. Decoding a confusing text from an ex, or calming down before a difficult conversation with a boss. AI becomes a non-judgmental prep space.

Risk
Real Harm Has Happened

High-profile cases of AI encouraging delusional beliefs and suicidal thoughts have emerged. General-purpose chatbots are not trained therapists — and the line between helpful support and dangerous influence can be invisible until it's crossed.

AI at Work: Three Tiers of Impact

63 of the top 100 use cases are work-related — but most of the impact is modest, and much of it is happening quietly without management knowing.

Efficiency

Most AI work use today is about doing the same things faster. Low risk, immediate payoff, easy to justify.

  • Summarizing meetings and notes
  • First drafts of requirements
  • Explaining data in plain language
  • Faster code reviews

Growth

Some teams use AI to drive new revenue. Concrete ROI numbers are still rare, but one example cited 20–30% conversion lifts from AI-optimized email campaigns.

  • Hyper-personalized marketing
  • Continuous A/B testing
  • AI-assisted sales outreach

Transformation

True business reinvention via AI is still early-stage and mostly happening at small companies and solo founders — not large enterprises.

  • New business models built with AI
  • Replacing entire functions
  • Autonomous agentic workflows

Real Voices from the Study

What actual users said — the clever, the honest, and the quietly alarming.

Example
The Shadow Worker

"I'm closing tickets 2x faster… got praised in my last performance review. But here's the thing: nobody knows I'm using AI."

Example
The Meaningless Loop

"I'm using AI to write my self-eval. My manager is using AI to review me. They'll put it all together and output something that reads nicely and means absolutely nothing."

Example
The Smart User

"I use AI all the time to poke holes in my own arguments — then I assess what I'm missing and refine it myself. AI is a mirror — not a genie. Use it as such."

Example
The Secret Agent Builder

"I built an AI agent to replace 50% of my job, secretly. Management didn't buy my proposal to do it openly — so now I do it quietly and spend the spare time on my own side business."

⚠️ Shadow usage creates invisible risk

Many employees use AI secretly to avoid policy violations or judgment. This means companies can't govern, improve, or even measure AI use they don't know is happening.

⚠️ AI emotional support is not professional therapy

Despite good intentions and safety guardrails, general-purpose chatbots lack clinical training. High-profile cases show AI has encouraged dangerous thinking in vulnerable users seeking emotional support.

⚠️ Most business AI benefits are incremental, not transformational

The research finds lots of modest wins — faster emails, tidier notes. Fundamental business reinvention via AI remains rare, early-stage, and mostly limited to small companies and founders.

"How much of our agency can survive such a powerful, ubiquitous, always-on service? Some of that will be up to the AI companies. But most is still up to us." — Marc Zao-Sanders
Key Takeaway

AI usage in 2026 is broader, deeper, and more intimate than ever — but this study's most urgent finding isn't about what AI can do. It's about what we risk losing: the habit of thinking for ourselves, the safety of emotional boundaries, and the honesty of genuine work. The technology is remarkable — whether it sharpens or dulls us depends entirely on how deliberately we choose to use it.
